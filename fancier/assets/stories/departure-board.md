# Stop 73: A Bus Departure Sign That Defined My Career

Three solar-powered bus departure signs run at Pioneer Valley Transit Authority stops in Western Massachusetts: Haigis Mall at UMass Amherst (stop 73), the Holyoke Public Library (stop 414), and the corner of Mill and Locust in Springfield (stop 1670). I built, wired, automated, and still service all of them. The first has been running in the field since August 2023, the full fleet since August 2025. Inside each one: an nRF9160 Feather running Zephyr RTOS talking over LTE Cat M1, a carrier board and LED display panels I designed from scratch, a single 100Ah self-heating LFP battery, and a 55W solar panel.

The project arrived in a Teams message from my boss Adam on October 21, 2021: "Might have an interesting project coming your way soon: PVTA wants to reverse engineer the hardware for the solar-powered real-time display at Haigis Mall. They also have them deployed in Holyoke, Eastfield, and Springfield. All are going to stop working this month as the company is no longer supporting them (the one at Haigis hasn't worked correctly all semester)."

The old signs ran on PIC microcontrollers with proprietary firmware built around 3G modems and CAN bus communication. AT&T's 3G shutdown had a date on the calendar: February 2022. The Eastfield sign was already gone; the mall closed and the sign was removed, so I inherited a fleet of three. The agency was unhappy with the quotes for new signs and my boss said "Justin can do that." That was the whole hiring process for this project. I was fresh out of college, an hourly, non-benefited W2 employee, paid out of a capital-projects budget.

By late November 2021, three months before the network went dark, I had been out to the sign with a tape measure and had picked up spare parts. The visible display window was about 138mm by 73mm; the biggest panel that would fit flush in the box was 158 by 89. I sent a parts list, a request for a SIM card, a plan, and a sentence I stand behind: "The dev time is honestly a bit of a wild guess since I haven't worked with a lot of the technology before."

The first controller I chose for the project was the MicroPython-based Pycom GPy. What I would call "Arduino class hardware" with an on-module LTE modem. I figured MicroPython would be easier to maintain down the line than C; I was so naive back then. It failed to reliably talk to the displays (among other issues), so it was out. Its replacement was a Blues Notecard and Swan MCU (with CircuitPython support).

Then came the part nobody warns you about when speaking over cellular: the data plan. The transit authority's original ask was ten seconds of latency, and at 30-second polling the signs still needed roughly 1.15GB each per month.

My verdict on the Blues starter kit went to Adam a day after it arrived: "Bad news, there is no way to circumvent Blues' Notehub which currently costs $0.006 everytime a single sign checks for departure time." When Blues came back with a $15,000 bulk-credit pitch, I ran their numbers and got them wrong by an order of magnitude. I like to teach computers how to do math so I don't have to. Adam caught it: "The numbers you sent aren't quite adding up... what am I missing?" The credits would last 19.29 years across 5 signs, not the 7.7 months I'd told the CIO. But my mind was already made up. A contract with a vendor whose platform didn't let me speak directly to my data sources wasn't going to be part of this project. We returned the kit.

I switched to a more complex microcontroller, the Circuit Dojo nRF9160 Feather (for some reason I was convinced the Adafruit Feather footprint was necessary). That meant I had to write the firmware in C on Zephyr, but at least we could use our own SIM cards, and the network stack was mine to control.

Now I was back to pricing out cellular data.

AT&T, who PVTA already had a contract for FirstNet with, told us the hardware was "not compatible". Verizon wanted $22 per device per month ($10 if we could use less than 1GB); Hologram (the SIM the board came with) worked out to about $82/month. My recommendation (shockingly) was Verizon, but PVTA was wary of signing a contract with another cellular data provider. From June to mid-August 2022 development was blocked waiting on a carrier decision.

Then AT&T's FirstNet team reversed itself on the compatibility issue, and a test SIM was physically dropped off at the UMass Transit dispatch window in December 2022. A certification wall followed in 2023: the board isn't PTCRB certified, and its maker reasonably declined to certify user-programmable hardware. Yet the data continued to flow and the fleet still runs on FirstNet today.

The project also nearly stopped for a non-technical reason. In November 2022 I had to tell the transit authority my UMass contract expired in December, and I quoted them the Massachusetts conflict of interest law: "After you leave your state job, you may never work for anyone other than the state on a matter that you worked on as a state employee." The contract got renewed instead. A proposal to finish the work under my own company was later rejected too; keeping me hourly W2 was easier and probably cheaper. From mid-2024 my project hours stepped down to thirty a week, then twenty, then ten on a monthly schedule. The retrofit got done anyway, on an hourly position that was never supposed to survive its first year.

March 1, 2023: first working prototype. Its countdown read exactly one minute off the existing web app, because the web app rounded up and I rounded down. I kept it rounded down. It's the correct behavior for a departure countdown.

A new problem surfaced. The off-the-shelf displays were too dim for direct sunlight and low in stock, so I designed my own LED panel PCBs. The first design took about a week and let us return $566.28 worth of store-bought panels. The retrofitted sign went up at Haigis Mall on August 28, 2023, and the field started teaching me far more than a bench prototype ever could.

Adam occasionally drives the Route 38 bus through Haigis Mall, so for two months he was my monitoring system: "How's my sign?" "Off again." When he reported it off again the following Monday, I asked if he'd tried kicking it. The real culprit was the batteries, or rather, power draw. Each sign ran four 20Ah lithium packs in parallel (80Ah total); I consolidated each sign to a single 100Ah self-heating unit with Bluetooth monitoring, after rejecting a dual-battery scheme on BMS grounds. My purchasing rule from that episode: "If a manufacturer requires that we use their own charger we should probably avoid them in case they want to deny a warranty claim." That solved the capacity issue, but not our charge rate. We couldn't generate enough with our solar panels to keep up with the demand.

The power problem got solved sideways. Instead of a bigger solar panel (the easy solution), which the transit authority's IT architect called "potentially a nightmare", and which would have needed university facilities approval for wind load, I redesigned the LED panels.

I designed the first panels to be dumb. Just a grid of WS2815E "Intelligent control LED"s (more popularly known as neopixels). I chose the WS2815E because it ran on 12V DC, the same as our batteries, no voltage conversion needed. But my design had two major issues: the design that let the WS2815E run on ~12V instead of ~5V of the more popular WS2812* gave the chip a *quiescent current of 2.1mA*, and I had more neopixels than I needed thanks to the grid. Those panels drew a constant ~3.2W per panel *with no LEDs on*, and ~5 watts at full brightness.

Back to the drawing board. The first revision, still using the WS2815E, was getting rid of the grid and arranging the LEDs as a 3-digit seven-segment display. No more wasted pixels, but the quiescent current was still a big issue.

The neopixels didn't feel particularly production grade either; the WS2815E price was high and stock was low. So I pivoted; the neopixels were replaced with dumb WS2850Bs and an IS31FL3733 to drive them. That meant I needed to add an on-panel buck converter to drop the voltage to the required 5V. The IS31FL3733 also spoke I2C instead of NZR, so a firmware change was needed.

The new panels looked promising, one more LED per digit and 3W at maximum brightness. At 20% brightness the new panels should draw 0.48 watts and ~0 when idle. Now the existing 55W panel would be enough.

The design was ready on day one, but paying for those panels took nine weeks of purchase orders, an out-of-stock part, a cancelled pre-order, and a consignment order through the board house's sister company.

Then the three test IS31FL3733 panels arrived... and failed. I spent some time debugging, testing with data buffers, checking the signals on the oscilloscope, etc. The data signals looked good and the firmware was speaking with the IS31FL3733s fine. Could have been bad components, or because the LEDs were never baked to remove moisture before assembly. In the end, tracking down the issue wasn't worth the time. I needed a solution to our power problem, and I needed it ASAP.

That's when I created the WS2813B-V5-based displays the signs use today. I already knew bitbanging "NZR" data via the SPI driver worked; that's also why the carrier board has a multiplexer. The Feather doesn't have enough SPI pins for every panel on its own and bitbanging in assembly wasn't working out (I should revisit that someday). But I kept the layout and buck converter of the IS31FL3733 panels. That meant I didn't need to upsize the power wires from the control box to the display boxes. In fact, it meant power for the displays could continue to come straight from the battery (with some protection in between). The buck converter IC also had an enable pin, which meant the controller could shut the whole panel down if it wasn't needed. Quiescent current problem solved; less watts at lower brightness too.

August 2024 was the proof: "Everything is working correctly with the Haigis Mall sign, and it's been running happily for a few weeks." I ordered 50 more LED boards for the rest of the fleet, $413.51 quoted, $373.33 final.

Of course, the signs kept finding new ways to die. December 2024: water got in the Holyoke sign and shorted the backlight wires at the bottom of the frame. "All the other hardware survived thanks to the circuit protections in place." The drowned battery lied about itself, reading full for four seconds at 4% charge, and was rescued with a battery charger in repair-mode.

January 2025 taught me the ugliest failure chain in the fleet: a battery short resets the microcontroller over and over until the modem trips into protection mode "and stops it from connecting to the network until it's powered off for 30 minutes." The protection mode continued to make other issues harder to discern. It was all the logs showed every time I plugged in, but it wasn't the root cause of the resets in the first place.

April 2025, Adam watched the Haigis sign die and resurrect in front of him: "Yep it turned off for a bit while I was here and came back on. Wonder if it's overheating?" The plexiglass was bowing outward on both sides; the electronics were cooking inside their own bubble. All the components used were rated to handle the heat, but you never know what the real world will give you. Multiple checks were made, but the hardware was always functioning. It had to be a firmware issue.

Every time a sign failed it meant driving to it, setting up a ladder, removing the 20 or so hex bolts holding the plexiglass cover on, opening the control box, dragging my laptop and programmer up the ladder, capturing logs, and reflashing the firmware. If the modem was in protection mode I had to swap the controller, or if I didn't have a spare on me, wait 30 minutes for the protection to clear.

The signs needed a redesign. Water ingress was fogging up the plexiglass and accessing the controller took way too long. Plus if a route changed the whole plexiglass panel needed to be reprinted.

There was some disagreement on how to handle the water issue. I wanted to add PTFE membrane vents and maybe a drain hole to let the water escape. The transit authority's IT architect wanted to seal the signs up water-tight. In the end I conceded on that issue. A clear water-tight plexiglass layer was added in front of the displays. Plexiglass slats for each route in front of that, and a control box outside the frame with a conduit connecting the two. The design changes meant a full re-wire, but now getting to the controller was much easier. In a twelve-hour day that May we swapped in a six-stop sign at Haigis Mall with the new design.

Later the same was done for the Mill & Locust sign after a car had struck it. On that one I made my favorite kind of mistake, publicly: I diagnosed the Springfield sign's "blinking" (resetting) as a strike-damaged control board. I replaced the board but the sign was still clearly resetting. I drove home, set my bench sign to that stop with full debug logging, then found the real issue. The cause was my own software cap of 6 routes; the real-time API was returning 7 simultaneous routes. I retracted my diagnosis the same day.

That sign only displayed 3 routes, and had up to 3 regularly scheduled routes depending on the time and day. I set the max at 6 to be safe. However there was another kind of bus that could service the stop. We call them "trippers". Tripper service is defined in 49 CFR § 605.3. But basically, trippers are buses that are scheduled when demand is higher than regular service can supply. They run the same route but are scheduled as needed under a separate name. I drove buses and dispatched for PVTA. I should have known about the trippers. But I forgot to account for it in my firmware.

That was the main issue causing the signs to reset for over a year by that point. I would have found the issue the first time it happened if I had access to the logs. But the log history was wiped out every time the watchdog reset the device and there wasn't enough space to store logs on board; no SD card slot either. I had nowhere to send the logs and even if I did, that's a lot of extra data usage for an occasional issue.

So, 3 scheduled routes and 4 tripper buses running at once. 3 + 4 = 7. 7 > 6. I raised the cap to 8, drove back to the sign, and flashed the new firmware. Long-standing issue solved.

Under all of this ran a fight with the cellular stack. My real-time data endpoint moved to a server requiring TLS 1.2 with 2048-bit RSA certs. The modem's connect call started failing with EAGAIN. I chased a heap-exhaustion theory through broken debug builds at 85.43% flash and 96.73% RAM, reproduced the failure with the vendor's own unmodified sample, and finally got a modem trace showing a duplicate ACK during the handshake. The fix that held was raising the socket connect timeout to 15 seconds: "10 seconds still has the same issue, 15 seconds seems okay so far but isn't ideal." That's up to half of my polling budget spent waiting to connect, and I never called it a real fix. But at least it was working.

By August 2025 the fleet was complete: the Holyoke Library and Springfield signs redeployed on the new hardware, Haigis on its six-stop frame. Only the rare reset caused by TLS issues.

What never existed, through four and a half years of water, heat, dead batteries, certification walls, and purchase orders, was a way to see any of it without driving to the sign. In May 2026 confirming the Haigis sign still meant Adam watching a bus on the tracker and calling the radio operator as it pulled in: "Haigis sign is working."

The monitoring discussion happened back in November of 2024. The transit authority's IT architect wanted to know when a sign went dark, and so did I. I admitted I'd already built prototype server software for simple data collection and OTA firmware upgrades, but I had nowhere to deploy it. What he wanted was an email to the helpdesk when a battery ran low. Both required a server, and nobody wanted to be responsible for managing said server. Adam said it in so many words: "Anyway, I don't want to run a middleware server."

I had a working system and no way to remotely manage it.

The off-the-shelf IoT vendors billed per request; against devices reporting every 30 seconds, the price was too high. I had been building my own software anyways, in pieces, since 2022. A Python repo, then a C-on-NGINX-Unit middleware I called "Loft", forked into the UMTS GitHub org to serve the signs (the server with nowhere to deploy it).

Finally, I made my own platform built in Rust and deployed on the Cloudflare edge. The signs run on it now; it's called PidgeIoT, and the fleet was its first tenant.
