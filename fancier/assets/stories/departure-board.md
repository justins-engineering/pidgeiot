# Stop 73; A Bus Departure Sign That Defined My Career

Three solar-powered bus departure signs run at Pioneer Valley Transit Authority stops in Western Massachusetts: Haigis Mall at UMass Amherst (stop 73), the Holyoke Public Library (stop 414), and the corner of Mill and Locust in Springfield (stop 1670). I built, wired, and automated all of them, and they have been in the field for about two years. Inside each one: an nRF9160 Feather running Zephyr RTOS over LTE Cat M1, a carrier board and LED display panels I designed from scratch, a single 100Ah self-heating battery, and the original 55W solar panel, which never needed replacing.

The project arrived in a Teams message from my boss Adam on October 21, 2021: "Might have an interesting project coming your way soon: PVTA wants to reverse engineer the hardware for the solar-powered real-time display at Haigis Mall. They also have them deployed in Holyoke, Eastfield, and Springfield. All are going to stop working this month as the company is no longer supporting them (the one at Haigis hasn't worked correctly all semester)." The old signs ran custom, proprietary controllers built around 3G modems, and AT&T's 3G shutdown had a date on the calendar: February 2022. The Eastfield sign was already gone; the mall closed and took it, so the fleet I inherited was three. The agency was unhappy with the signs and my boss said "Justin can do that." That was the whole hiring process. I was fresh out of college, an hourly, non-benefited W2 employee, paid out of a capital-projects budget.

By late November 2021, three months before the network went dark, I had been out to the sign with a tape measure. The visible display window was about 138mm by 73mm; the biggest panel that would fit flush in the box was 158 by 89. I sent a parts list, a prototype plan, a request for an LTE-M SIM card, and a sentence I stand behind: "The dev time is honestly a bit of a wild guess since I haven't worked with a lot of the technology before."

The first controller was MicroPython and Arduino class hardware with a separate modem. It failed to reliably talk to the displays. Its replacement had a pricing model that made it financially unfeasible. So I switched to a more complex microcontroller, the nRF9160 Feather, and wrote the firmware in C on Zephyr.

Then came the part nobody warns you about: the data plan. The transit authority's original ask was ten seconds of latency, and at 30-second polling the signs still needed roughly 1.15GB each per month. We bought a Blues starter kit; my verdict went to Adam a day after it arrived: "Bad news, there is no way to circumvent Blues' Notehub which currently costs $0.006 everytime a single sign checks for departure time." We returned the kit. Verizon wanted $22 per device per month, Hologram worked out to about $82.62, and from June to mid-August 2022 development was fully blocked waiting on a carrier decision. When Blues came back with a $15,000 bulk-credit pitch, I ran their numbers and got them wrong by an order of magnitude. Adam caught it: "The numbers you sent aren't quite adding up... what am I missing?" The credits would last 19.29 years across 5 signs, not the 7.7 months I'd told the CIO. Then AT&T's FirstNet, which had told us the hardware was "not compatible," reversed itself, and a test SIM was physically dropped off at the UMass Transit dispatch window in December 2022. A certification wall followed in 2023: the board isn't PTCRB certified, and its maker reasonably declined to certify user-programmable hardware. And yet the fleet runs on FirstNet today, on new SIM cards.

The project also nearly stopped for a non-technical reason. In November 2022 I had to tell the transit authority my UMass contract expired in December, and I quoted them the Massachusetts conflict of interest law: "After you leave your state job, you may never work for anyone other than the state on a matter that you worked on as a state employee." The contract got renewed instead. A $163,000 proposal to finish the work under my own company was later rejected too; keeping me hourly W2 was easier and probably cheaper, and from mid-2024 my project hours stepped down to thirty a week, then twenty, then ten. The retrofit got done anyway, on an hourly position that was never supposed to survive its first year.

March 1, 2023: first working prototype. Its countdown read exactly one minute off the existing web app, because the web app rounded up and I rounded down. I kept it rounded down. It's the correct behavior for a departure countdown.

The off-the-shelf displays were too dim for direct sunlight and low in stock, so I designed my own LED panel PCBs. The design took about a week and let us return $566.28 worth of store-bought panels. The retrofitted sign went up at Haigis Mall on August 28, 2023, and the field started teaching me things. Adam drives the Route 38 bus through Haigis Mall, so for two months he was my monitoring system: "How's my sign?" "Off again." When he reported it off again the following Monday, I asked if he'd tried kicking it. The real culprit was the batteries. Each sign ran four 20Ah lithium packs; I consolidated to a single 100Ah self-heating unit with Bluetooth monitoring, after rejecting a dual-battery scheme on BMS grounds. My purchasing rule from that episode: "If a manufacturer requires that we use their own charger we should probably avoid them in case they want to deny a warranty claim."

The power problem got solved sideways. Instead of a bigger solar panel, which the transit authority's IT architect called "potentially a nightmare", and which would have needed university facilities approval for wind load, I redesigned the LED panels. The old design drew 5.04 watts at full brightness; the new one, with one more LED per digit, drew 3. At 20% brightness the new panels drew 0.48 watts against the old 3, and suddenly the existing 55W panel was enough. Three IS31FL3733 driver chips, 21 RGB LEDs per digit, 189 LEDs total, mapped by hand in a spreadsheet one afternoon. Paying for those panels took nine weeks of purchase orders, an out-of-stock part, a cancelled pre-order, and a consignment order through the board house's sister company. The design was ready on day one.

Then the three IS31FL3733 panels failed. I spent some time debugging, testing data buffers, checking the signals on the oscilloscope. Could have been bad components, or because the LEDs were never baked. In the end I decided tracking down the issue wasn't worth the time. That's when I created the WS2813B-V5 based displays the signs use today. I already knew bitbanging "NZR" data via the SPI driver worked; that's also why the carrier board has a multiplexer, because the Feather doesn't have enough SPI pins on its own.

August 2024 was the proof: "Everything is working correctly with the Haigis Mall sign, and it's been running happily for a few weeks." I ordered 50 LED boards for the rest of the fleet, $413.51 quoted, $373.33 final.

The signs kept finding new ways to die. December 2024: water got in at Holyoke and shorted the backlight wires at the bottom of the frame. "All the other hardware survived thanks to the circuit protections in place." The drowned battery lied about itself, reading full for four seconds at 4% charge, and was rescued with a repair-mode charger, 25% to 100% at 99.8Ah. January 2025 taught me the ugliest failure chain in the fleet: a battery short resets the microcontroller over and over until the modem trips into protection mode "and stops it from connecting to the network until it's powered off for 30 minutes." April 2025, Adam watched the Haigis sign die and resurrect in front of him: "Yep it turned off for a bit while I was here and came back on. Wonder if it's overheating?" The plexiglass was bowing outward on both sides; the electronics were cooking inside their own bubble, possibly because I'd made the enclosure too airtight when I re-tapped the screw holes. We swapped in a new six-stop sign in a twelve-hour day that May. And I made my favorite kind of mistake, publicly: I diagnosed the Springfield sign's blinking as a strike-damaged control board and replaced the board, then retracted it the same day. The real cause was my own software cap of 6 routes being hit by 7 simultaneous routes when 4 tripper buses run at once. I raised the cap to 8.

Under all of this ran a fight with the cellular stack. The data endpoint moved to a server demanding TLS 1.2 with 2048-bit RSA certs, and the modem's connect call started failing with EAGAIN. I chased a heap-exhaustion theory through broken debug builds at 85.43% flash and 96.73% RAM, reproduced the failure with the vendor's own unmodified sample, and finally got a modem trace showing a duplicate ACK during the handshake. The fix that held was raising the socket connect timeout to 15 seconds: "10 seconds still has the same issue, 15 seconds seems okay so far but isn't ideal." That's a third of my polling budget spent waiting to connect, and I never called it a real fix.

By August 2025 the fleet was complete: the Holyoke Library and Springfield signs redeployed on the new hardware, Haigis on its six-stop frame. What never existed, through four and a half years of water, heat, dead batteries, certification walls, and purchase orders, was a way to see any of it without driving to the sign. In May 2026 confirming the Haigis sign still meant Adam watching a bus on the tracker and calling the radio operator as it pulled in: "Haigis sign is working."

The monitoring argument happened in November 2024. The transit authority's IT architect wanted to know when a sign went dark; I admitted I'd already built prototype server software for data collection and firmware upgrades and never deployed it. What he wanted was an email to the helpdesk when a battery ran low. The off-the-shelf IoT vendors billed per request, against a device reporting every 30 seconds, and nobody wanted to run a middleware server. Adam said it in so many words: "Anyway, I don't want to run a middleware server." I had been building one anyway, in pieces, since 2022: a Python repo whose last commit is titled "Feather code", then a C-on-NGINX-Unit middleware whose README was already titled "# Loft", forked into the university's GitHub org to serve the signs.

I had a working system and no way to remotely manage it. So I made my own platform. The signs run on it now; it's called PidgeIoT, and the fleet was its first tenant.
